# Sensitivity check: single-player systemic games as ECS consumers

This third-round check tests whether a single-player immersive sim or survival game can keep the cross-family interactions that make Space Station 14 compelling without requiring station multiplayer. It compares *Streets of Rogue*, *Spelunky*, *Don't Starve*, and *Rain World*. Gameplay statements below come from developers, publishers, or developer-authored materials. The ECS mappings and recommendation are design inferences. None of these sources establishes that the four games use ECS internally.

## Finding

Among these four, the strongest **concrete first clone for a broad ECS showcase** is a small *Streets of Rogue*-inspired, single-player mission level, provided its actor–tool–target interaction is designed around Bendvy's actual access boundaries. Its official description makes the core explicitly solo-capable and defines play around a mission goal that can be solved through character traits, items, and the environment. In a dated developer announcement, Matt Dabrowski contrasts the first game's simple level-based structure, which supported small vertical slices, with the sequel's larger open-world systems. That makes one mission level a grounded scope choice for a clone, not a claim that the source game itself has tiny scope. ([July 18, 2024 developer announcement](https://steamcommunity.com/games/512900/announcements/detail/4354501493553620255))

This is a feasibility compromise, not a claim of maximum ECS breadth. *Rain World* is the strongest ecological-simulation challenger: its developer describes creatures acting independently, seeking food and shelter, hunting each other, remembering harm, and forming trust. That is a richer multi-agent world, but its movement, creature AI, animation, room connectivity, and content can consume the project before the ECS consumer is playable. *Streets of Rogue* is the more defensible first clone because its level-and-objective structure gives us a closed slice and its solo mode does not remove the game’s central play pattern.

For the **highest architecture evidence**, SS14 still leads because its official engine documentation and source show data components and entity systems. *Baba Is You* remains the clearest test case for player-caused runtime behavior changes at low host cost, though its source materials do not describe an ECS implementation. *FTL* remains the best fixed-layout example for crew ownership, tactical phases, and a finished ship run. SoR is a promising compromise: more entity-family interaction than FTL or Baba, with a smaller closed slice than SS14, but no public source evidence that its original implementation is ECS.

## Candidate comparison

| Reference | Natural reusable composition | Closed recognizable slice | Main work outside ECS |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *Streets of Rogue* | Player and NPCs can share actor, faction, health, inventory, and status data; player control, AI intent, awareness, and interaction can be separate capabilities where independently useful. Items act on several actor and prop families. Mission and alert systems observe interactions. | One block, one mission, one exit. The player retrieves an item from a guarded safe and escapes; hacking, stealth, social distraction, or force remain available. Extraction is success; incapacitation and the prototype's explicitly custom alarm meter can provide fail states. | NPC perception/decision rules, line of sight, tile collision and destructible props, mission content, and balancing. Keep generation to a fixed authored block at first. |
| *Spelunky* | Player, enemies, pickups, traps, and thrown objects can share position, collision, damage, and lifetime behavior; object use and destruction cross entity families. | One short randomized cavern level with a key/objective and exit is recognizable. | Platform physics, destructible terrain, collision timing, trap chains, and platform movement feel. This risks becoming a physics/level-generation demo rather than an ECS consumer. |
| *Don't Starve* | Health, hunger, temperature, wetness, burning, inventory, and perishability can be separate capabilities consumed by different systems. Food/resources, creatures, fire, and structures can react through shared damage/status or item rules. | Gather wood/grass, craft an axe and campfire, then survive the first night; death or dawn closes the slice. This preserves the original survival loop without a campaign. | Resource/world generation, many recipes and item behaviors, time/weather, creature AI, and balancing. Recipes and character differences can stay static content data rather than entities. |
| *Rain World* | Creatures can combine movement, needs, predator/prey preferences, threat/friend/item trackers, shelter goals, and carried items. Relationship and target references can change while common movement, damage, hunger, and death systems still apply. | One room-cycle: gather enough food, evade or exploit one predator/prey encounter, reach shelter before the rain deadline. | Slippery physics/platforming, procedural creature animation, room graph/pathfinding, autonomous AI, predator-prey tuning, and the rain/hibernation loop. A coarse movement model may stop feeling like Rain World. |

The *Streets of Rogue* model has the best balance because **its independent families have player-visible alternatives**. A player doesn't merely fight a larger crowd: they can change how an NPC or object behaves, use one object to affect another, and still complete the mission. The ECS opportunity is to give those actions coherent owners and let distinct systems select relevant capabilities across actor and prop families. The model does not require a separate marker for every field, status, or trait: a cohesive `ActorStatus` struct, a `LockState` enum, a status bitset, or a local `match` over an item's `UseAction` can all be reasonable. A granularity failure would be one universal resolver that owns unrelated AI, movement, inventory, hacking, damage, status, and cleanup behavior, not the presence of enums, flags, or local branches.

## Proposed SoR-like ECS scenario

This is an original design proposal, not a reconstruction of SoR internals. Use one hand-authored block with a guard, a worker, a camera, a locked vault, a terminal, a sedative item, a valuable, and an exit. The player must get the valuable and leave. A guard can raise an alarm; alarm pressure makes stealth and social distraction consequential. The player can hack the camera or lock, distract or sedate the guard, or force the door. Incapacitation is defeat. **For this proposed mission only, a full alarm meter also triggers defeat; this is a custom closed-slice rule, not a claim about original Streets of Rogue.** Reaching the exit with the item is victory.

The example component names below describe possible query/data boundaries, not a mandatory one-marker-per-field schema. Group values that share an invariant or owner—for example, `ActorStatus{...}`, `LockState{Locked, Unlocked}`, `PowerState{...}`, or a finite `UseAction` enum. Keep capabilities separate where different systems or entity families independently own or consume them. A discriminant match local to interpreting `UseAction` or a target state is acceptable; the design test is whether one general handler becomes responsible for unrelated gameplay rules.

| Family | Example composition | Independent systems that use it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Player, guard, worker | `Position`, `Collider`, `Health`, `Faction`, `Actor`; optional `PlayerControl`, `AIIntent`, `Inventory`, `Awareness`, `Breathes`, `Stunnable` | Movement/collision; common damage and statuses; AI perception/intent only for AI actors; interaction; inventory and item ownership; mission checks. |
| Terminal, camera, door, vault | `Position`, `Interactable`; optional `Hackable`, `PowerConsumer`, `Lockable`, `Damageable`, `AlarmSource`, `LootContainer` | Hack, power, lock, damage, alarm, and objective systems each query only the capability or state they own. A door can be lockable and damageable; a camera can be powered and hackable; target data determines the applicable work. A local enum may still choose among outcomes within one interaction system. |
| Sedative spray or dart | `Item`, `UseAction`, `StatusOnHit`, `Lifetime` if projectile; `HeldBy`/`FiredBy` relation as appropriate | Inventory, delivery, collision, status, attribution, and cleanup. |
| Stolen valuable | `Item`, `MissionObjective`, `Position`; `HeldBy` after pickup | Pickup/transfer, ownership cleanup, and objective/exit checks. |
| Mission state | Resource: `AlertLevel`, objective state, level clock, seed | AI scheduling, victory/defeat evaluation, UI. Do not turn fixed objectives or catalog rows into entities without a lifecycle need. |

The composition counterfactual is a sedative dart that changes behavior for player-controlled and AI-controlled characters through shared actor/status data, not separate player and guard handlers. If consciousness has an independent lifetime and several systems need to select only unconscious actors, represent it as an optional `Unconscious{expires_at}` capability. If statuses are one cohesive actor-owned value, keep them together in `ActorStatus{unconscious_until, ...}` and update that state in place; both can be sound ECS designs. Player-input and NPC-decision systems consume the common status state according to their own control/AI responsibilities. The camera is excluded because the effect targets actors with the relevant status/susceptibility data, not because a central kind switch special-cases it. This example demonstrates composition only if independently owned hit, status-expiry, and control/AI systems actually use the shared state; the component's physical shape is not the test.

Treat `AlarmRaised` as an informational event with independent readers: the mission evaluator starts or advances the failure timer; nearby NPC perception changes intent; the HUD/log records why the level escalated. A `HeldBy(item → actor)` or `FiredBy(dart → actor)` relation has a concrete purpose in transfer, attribution, and cleanup. A state transition can update a cohesive `ActorStatus`, `LockState`, or inventory-state value in place. Use component insertion/removal and its explicit barrier only where optional membership has independent query or lifecycle value. Do not route the gameplay action itself through a buffered event if it needs a synchronous allow/deny result; use input/intent plus a validation result, then publish committed facts for observers.

An implementation that stores a dozen object kinds in one record and routes unrelated movement, AI, interaction, inventory, and damage rules through one growing universal `match kind { ... }` resolver would be a weak ECS case. A local enum branch or a cohesive flags/state payload is not a failure by itself. Likewise, static `Hacker`, `Guard`, or `Doctor` trait IDs are valid content data but do not by themselves demonstrate runtime composition. The proof should be observable in player behavior and system ownership: for example, the same status data changes how both player-control and NPC-decision systems behave, while distinct alarm readers independently update mission state and presentation. Record structural membership changes separately; do not require every gameplay state change to add or remove a marker component.

## Bendvy API fit: actor–tool–target join

The current access audit materially qualifies this candidate. In the ordinary App query path, a writer addresses the current entity row. A relation view can return a detached target handle, but it does not grant reads of that target's components; a write to an explicit other handle uses a deferred command and becomes visible at a barrier. A short standard query that starts from an actor, follows its held-item relation, reads a different target family, then journal-writes actor, item, and target together is not available. ([Cross-entity API audit](12-cross-entity-access-audit.md))

This is a limitation of the convenient ordinary route, not an absolute claim that the behavior cannot be implemented. The audit identifies workable options: precompute an immutable action plan and process each writable family in separate typed passes; use deferred explicit-target commands and accept their barrier semantics; or build a wider lower-level `Ops`/query adapter and separately validate its grants and rollback behavior. None gives a blanket all-or-nothing guarantee spanning the actor, held item, target, and a later structural barrier. That matters here because the player's defining action is exactly “use this item/tool on that other entity.”

Therefore the proposed slice must either define staged interaction semantics—reserve/consume the item only when the target update is confirmed, and expose any intermediate state explicitly—or first prove a composite adapter. If that integration boundary dominates the player-visible loop, the SoR recommendation weakens: Baba's same-family push updates or a Magicka-style same-family hit plan are a better fit for the current standard query path. The research does not assume a generic free cross-entity capability.

## Direct comparison with SS14, FTL, and Baba

| Candidate | What it does better than this SoR-like slice | What SoR does better for the requested clone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **SS14** | Its official ECS guide and public code demonstrate the architecture directly: data components, entity systems, cross-cutting damage/tools, containment, power and atmosphere. Its natural world has the broadest family/relation surface. ([SS14 ECS guide](https://docs.spacestation14.com/en/robust-toolbox/ecs.html); the pinned source details are in [study 07](07-space-station.md).) | A one-player mission is a native shape of SoR; a solo engineer emergency can preserve the station's technical work but drops its multiplayer/social role-play center. SoR retains a solo immersive-sim loop without pretending to be a full station. |
| **FTL** | It has cleaner tactical phases, explicit crew-to-room/system duties, ship ownership, and a closed multi-encounter run with fixed layouts. Its official page confirms pausing, crew orders, power allocation, and target selection. ([Subset Games on Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/212680/FTL_Faster_Than_Light/); see [study 08](08-modular-ships.md).) | SoR offers more cross-family object/actor solutions to one local mission and more runtime changes from hacking, statuses, item transfer, and alarms. It is less naturally bounded than FTL's encounter phases. |
| **Baba Is You** | It is the clearest and cheapest runtime-composition demonstration: a move changes a rule, which adds/removes behavior queried by movement, collision, and win systems. Creator materials describe the rule-question pattern, but do not show ECS. ([official game page](https://store.steampowered.com/app/736260/Baba_Is_You/), [creator's GDC slides](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc2020/presentations/Reading%20the%20rules_Teikari_Arvi.pdf); see [study 10](10-rule-puzzles.md).) | SoR makes relations, independent actors, inventory transfer, targeted effects, events, and multiple entity families more central. It has higher implementation cost and a less direct component-change trigger. |

Decision: **keep SS14 as the strongest documented ECS product and Baba as the strongest minimal dynamic-composition experiment. If choosing a concrete single-player systemic clone that borrows station-like cross-family interactions without stripping multiplayer from a multiplayer core, choose the SoR-like one-block mission.** FTL remains preferable if a safer fixed-layout, phase-heavy game is desired. Do not promote SoR to an ECS winner if its implementation depends on a centralized interaction/AI switch; in that case Baba has a cleaner causal demonstration and FTL has the cleaner bounded simulation.

## Conditions that would change the result

- Choose **Rain World** over SoR if the objective is autonomous multi-agent ecology and the team is willing to build the creature movement, perception, target relations, room navigation, and shelter/rain loop as the main project. It has the strongest developer-described agent relations in this set.
- Choose **Don't Starve** over SoR if a single-player survival loop is more important than open-ended NPC interaction. It has a very clear first-night slice; keep recipes as data and limit the world to a small authored region so resource simulation does not replace ECS work.
- Choose **Spelunky** over SoR if a compact action roguelike with pickup/throw/destruction is the goal and a custom platform-physics/terrain host is acceptable. It beats SoR on the compact run structure, not on independent cross-family system breadth.
- Prefer a **plain array model** for any of these if the small slice has fixed entity families, one central traversal remains clearest, and adding component queries only creates indirection.
- Do not infer that “simulation” or “AI modules” means ECS. First-party gameplay facts support design analysis; architecture claims need source code or an explicit engine/design description.
