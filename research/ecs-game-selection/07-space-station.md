# Candidate study: Space Station 13/14 as a Bendvy ECS showcase

**Hypothesis 7 of 10.** This study separates three things that are easy to conflate: the original SS13 code architecture, the ECS that SS14 actually uses, and a possible Bendvy model. The source inventory is primary-source research; all proposed components, systems, slice rules, and ratings below are design inferences. No game was implemented or benchmarked.

## Recommendation

**Space Station 14 is one of the strongest candidates for maximum ECS exposure.** Its ordinary play crosses many independently reusable mechanics: an entity can be movable, damageable, held or contained, tool-usable, atmos-exposed, powered, and network-visible at once. Damage, tools, hands, containers, atmosphere, power, construction, and round flow have distinct systems that can touch the same station and crew entities. The resulting ECS story is richer than adding more enemy or projectile kinds to the existing arena.

**It is a weak choice for the first small Bendvy clone unless the target is deliberately an original, single-player station-emergency game.** SS14 describes itself as a round-based multiplayer role-playing game where crew jobs, social interaction, chaos, and survival shape the shift. A solo engineer repairing life support can retain the recognizable station-work loop, but it removes much of the social role-play core. Even a compact technical slice carries a custom grid, collision, interaction UI, and a simplified atmosphere/power model; a faithful SS14 clone adds multiplayer, networking, authored content, and mature subsystem solvers.

So the two answers differ: **keep SS14 near the top for “most mechanisms that naturally compose through ECS”; keep it below the best small first-clone candidates for “small finished game on current Bendvy.”** This is an initial candidate assessment, not the overall selection.

## What the primary sources establish

The official SS14 page describes an ordinary station shift that goes wrong, with player-selected crew or enemy jobs, detailed station mechanics, role-play, and emergent choices during disasters and sabotage. That establishes the recognizable gameplay target and explains why the social core matters; it says nothing by itself about the engine architecture. ([About Space Station 14](https://spacestation14.com/about/about/))

The **original SS13 is not one codebase or one architecture**. As a concrete first-party sample, the current `/tg/station` branch uses a DM `/atom` object hierarchy and `/datum/component` objects that attach to a parent and react to its signals. Its component source calls this a way to add shared behavior independent of inheritance; it still gives the component behavior and a parent relationship, unlike the data-only component/system split described by SS14. Its mobs subsystem keeps a `mob_living` list and calls each mob’s `Life()` procedure. This sample supports “component-oriented, object/subsystem architecture,” not “SS13 is a modern data-oriented ECS.” It must not be generalized to every SS13 fork. ([`/tg/station` component datum](https://github.com/tgstation/tgstation/blob/b3949ec3629836fa25e3e5732d5dcefd2dc04c4d/code/datums/components/_component.dm#L1-L12), [`/tg/station` mobs subsystem](https://github.com/tgstation/tgstation/blob/b3949ec3629836fa25e3e5732d5dcefd2dc04c4d/code/controllers/subsystem/mobs.dm#L30-L45))

SS14 is the architectural contrast. Its [official ECS guide](https://docs.spacestation14.com/en/robust-toolbox/ecs.html) describes components as data/markers, entity systems as the home for behavior, and event subscriptions as one route between systems. The guide explicitly distinguishes directed events on one entity from broadcast events, and documents immutable, cancellable, handled, and lifecycle events. It recommends composition for complex game objects. The actual [RobustToolbox source](https://github.com/space-wizards/RobustToolbox) identifies it as the engine used by SS14 and says the engine is being developed for both single-player and multiplayer projects.

This is evidence of a genuine entity-component-system design in SS14, but **not evidence that every part of the game is a pure, flat ECS or that it uses Bevy-style archetype storage**. Robust has engine-owned managers and services alongside components and entity systems. For example, the pinned `SharedContainerSystem` has a TODO to make `ContainerManagerComponent` ECS-native, so inventory/containment is partly a specialized engine service at this source revision. ([RobustToolbox `SharedContainerSystem.cs`, pinned source](https://github.com/space-wizards/RobustToolbox/blob/536b8ca955a361330990398e99282272aac974c1/Robust.Shared/Containers/SharedContainerSystem.cs#L21-L49), especially its TODO at [line 94](https://github.com/space-wizards/RobustToolbox/blob/536b8ca955a361330990398e99282272aac974c1/Robust.Shared/Containers/SharedContainerSystem.cs#L94))

The concrete code examples below are pinned to the `space-station-14` commit `971889f18a6b5f14b926d139ba3910b2872e14de`, RobustToolbox `536b8ca955a361330990398e99282272aac974c1`, and `/tg/station` `b3949ec3629836fa25e3e5732d5dcefd2dc04c4d`. Those pins make the specific code observations repeatable; the official wiki pages are live documentation and can change.

## Why the mechanics reward ECS composition

The strongest reusable behavior is **damage and destruction across unlike entity families**. In the pinned SS14 source, `DestructibleSystem` subscribes to a `DestructibleComponent` and a damage event, and depends on atmosphere, construction, explosions, gibbing, puddles, containers, chemical solutions, stacks, and triggers. This is a real integration seam: one destruction rule can cause station-level environmental change, entity cleanup, loot/material outcomes, and effects without making every target the same kind of object. ([`DestructibleSystem.cs`](https://github.com/space-wizards/space-station-14/blob/971889f18a6b5f14b926d139ba3910b2872e14de/Content.Server/Destructible/DestructibleSystem.cs#L28-L60))

Tools give a second strong cross-family pattern. SS14’s `ToolComponent` stores a set of tool qualities and speed/sound data. `SharedToolSystem` checks whether the tool has the required qualities, asks the user, tool, and target whether use is allowed, and lets those event handlers cancel the attempt. Thus the same wrench-like item can act on several target families that each interpret a shared tool-use interaction differently. This is a particularly clear composition example: the item is described by its capabilities; the target’s components and systems determine what repair or construction operation applies. ([`ToolComponent.cs`](https://github.com/space-wizards/space-station-14/blob/971889f18a6b5f14b926d139ba3910b2872e14de/Content.Shared/Tools/Components/ToolComponent.cs#L8-L43), [`SharedToolSystem.cs`](https://github.com/space-wizards/space-station-14/blob/971889f18a6b5f14b926d139ba3910b2872e14de/Content.Shared/Tools/Systems/SharedToolSystem.cs#L249-L303))

Containment creates a third family of shared behavior. A character’s hands, pockets, a toolbox, an emergency closet, a disposal unit, and a machine input all need rules about which entities they contain, how contents move, whether contents are visible, and what happens when a container is removed. Robust’s container manager listens to component lifecycle and parent-change events and shuts down its owned containers when that manager component is removed. The [SS14 hands system](https://github.com/space-wizards/space-station-14/blob/971889f18a6b5f14b926d139ba3910b2872e14de/Content.Server/Hands/Systems/HandsSystem.cs#L137-L183) also composes hands, container membership, action blocking, stack splitting, physics, and throwing. These rules are valuable ECS work, but the TODO above warns against treating every relation as a simple entity-to-entity pointer.

The final shared surface is a **living crew member inside a changing environment**. A player-controlled humanoid can participate in movement and physics, damage and mob-state updates, hands and inventory, breathing and temperature exposure, tool actions, and round-end evacuation. A station door or wall can participate in transform/collision, damage, airtightness, interaction, and power; a damaged door can affect air flow and emergency movement. SS14’s atmosphere system queries atmos-exposed entities with transforms, obtains their containing air mixture, and raises an exposure update; it also declares ordering after the node-group system and uses grid/map, airtight, firelock, container, physics, lookup, and damage dependencies. That is a strong cross-system workload, but it also shows how much specialized environmental machinery is hidden behind the ECS-facing entities. ([`AtmosphereSystem.cs`](https://github.com/space-wizards/space-station-14/blob/971889f18a6b5f14b926d139ba3910b2872e14de/Content.Server/Atmos/EntitySystems/AtmosphereSystem.cs#L29-L69), update and exposure loop at [lines 98–120](https://github.com/space-wizards/space-station-14/blob/971889f18a6b5f14b926d139ba3910b2872e14de/Content.Server/Atmos/EntitySystems/AtmosphereSystem.cs#L98-L120))

Power has the same pattern at a separate network boundary: `PowerNetSystem` subscribes to initialization, shutdown, pause, and unpause of receiver, consumer, and supplier components, then runs after `NodeGroupSystem`. This gives power nodes and device state another genuine component/system lifecycle, while leaving the connected-network solver as a specialized service. ([`PowerNetSystem.cs`](https://github.com/space-wizards/space-station-14/blob/971889f18a6b5f14b926d139ba3910b2872e14de/Content.Server/Power/EntitySystems/PowerNetSystem.cs#L21-L66))

The reusable scenario to carry forward is therefore not “many entities” alone. It is **one entity changing function as components and relations change, while several independent systems still recognize it**: a crew member picks up a tool from a container, repairs a powered airtight door, damage and tool state update, atmosphere begins to recover, and the round objective observes the result. A single `kind` switch could also implement this, but it would centralize exactly the cross-family rules that the ECS version should expose as independent systems.

## Proposed Bendvy mapping (design inference)

Do not copy SS14 class names as proof of fit. Model a small set of capabilities that overlap on purpose:

| Entity family | Candidate components/relations | Independent consumers |
|---|---|---|
| Engineer | `Transform`, `Velocity`, `PlayerControlled`, `MobState`, `Damageable`, `Breathes`, `Hands`, `Inventory`, `ToolUser`, `InRoom` | input/movement, tool eligibility, item transfer, breathing/exposure, health/death, objective/evacuation |
| Hand tool | `Item`, `ToolQualities`, `Durability`, `Transform`; a `HeldBy` or `Inside` relation | inventory, tool-use validation, repair/construction, drop/throw, damage/destruction |
| Airlock or powered machine | `Transform`, `Damageable`, `Interactable`, `Airtight`, `PowerConsumer`, `ToolTarget`, optional `Door`/`MachineState` | interaction, power, collision, damage, air-zone update, alarm and objective checks |
| Breach or damaged wall | `Transform`, `Damageable`, optional `Airtight`/`Destructible` | collision, damage, room-pressure update, fire/exposure, repair, removal cleanup |
| Shuttle/exit | `Transform`, `Container`/boarding relation, `EvacuationState`, `Interactable` | arrival timer, passenger/crew checks, victory/round-end, presentation |

These are names for a proposed Bendvy model, not claims about SS14’s exact component schema. Use shared pieces such as `Transform`, `Damageable`, `Interactable`, and `ToolQualities` across different families. Avoid one `StationEntityKind` component with a branch in movement, interaction, and damage. Use non-entity resources for the scenario clock, UI state, and small room/air-zone model unless the gameplay truly needs individual identity for those records.

For relations, use a typed relation or hierarchy for `HeldBy`/`Inside`, character control/body attachment, and any pipe or power connections that the slice actually needs. Keep transform parenting distinct from gameplay ownership when they mean different things. On item, container, target, or room deletion, choose the game rule explicitly: drop contents, transfer them, or delete them, then rely on Bendvy’s declared relation/hierarchy cleanup to preserve both ends. The current Bendvy relation work covers inverse consistency, deferred visibility, and deletion cleanup; see the local [relation contract](../../../bendvy/docs/parity/relations.md) and [hierarchy contract](../../../bendvy/docs/parity/hierarchy.md). A relation cleanup guarantee does not choose whether a bag spills its contents or a contained item is destroyed; that remains a game rule.

Three concrete additions can make composition visible without adding a new station-specific system to each interaction:

1. Give one repair tool a `ToolQualities + Item + Durability` composition and let it target both an airlock and an APC. A reusable tool-use validator and repair path can read shared `Repairable + Damageable` capabilities; `Airtight` and `PowerConsumer` determine which existing hazard or power queries change afterward. New target behavior is authored by composing data, not by adding separate `RepairDoor` and `RepairAPC` loops.
2. Put `Damageable + Repairable` on the engineer, wall, and APC. The same damage/repair work updates all three families. A broken wall loses `Airtight`; a broken APC loses or disables `PowerConsumer`; the engineer instead changes `MobState`. Those optional capabilities route the committed consequence into existing environmental, power, and health work.
3. Reuse one typed `Inside`/containment relation for a tool in the engineer’s belt, a fuse in an APC slot, and the engineer aboard the shuttle. Transfer and teardown use one relation lifecycle and deletion cleanup policy, while per-container data carries capacity/access rules. This tests actual containment and relation cleanup without a bespoke station relation table.

These are proposed generic interaction, damage, and containment facilities, not capabilities already proven by this application. The point is that the station-specific novelty sits in component combinations and rules for air and power, while shared systems remain independently useful in another game.

For observable system work, register a small explicit sequence through the ordinary App: accept player intent; validate tool/user/target and commit the repair or reject it without side effects; apply movement and hazard updates; process damage/status; flush structural changes at a deliberate barrier; clean up deaths or removed entities; then evaluate evacuation/victory and observe committed informational events. Bendvy’s [ordinary App contract](../../../bendvy/docs/reference/ordinary-app.md) says registrations run in order, structural commands require explicit barriers, and no automatic parallelism is promised. The [evaluation contract](00-evaluation-contract.md) also says host rendering, physics, collision, audio, input, navigation, and network replication are not supplied automatically.

### Directed event semantics do not port as buffered events

This difference is important enough to shape the model. Robust’s `RaiseLocalEvent` dispatches immediately to the local event bus; its entity-targeted overload can be directed to one UID and optionally broadcast as well. Robust also has a separate `QueueLocalEvent` API for events queued to the end of a tick. The SS14 ECS guide describes directed events as component-filtered callbacks on one entity, including lifecycle and cancellable attempt events. For instance, tool use can synchronously ask the user, item, and target whether to cancel before it happens. ([Robust `EntitySystem.cs`, immediate local raise methods](https://github.com/space-wizards/RobustToolbox/blob/536b8ca955a361330990398e99282272aac974c1/Robust.Shared/GameObjects/EntitySystem.cs#L122-L153), [directed local overloads](https://github.com/space-wizards/RobustToolbox/blob/536b8ca955a361330990398e99282272aac974c1/Robust.Shared/GameObjects/EntitySystem.cs#L218-L254))

Bendvy’s current informational `Data` events are retained buffered streams with independent reader cursors and publication governed by transaction commit. They are not synchronous callbacks, cancellable target queries, or an automatic replacement for lifecycle hooks. In the proposed slice, represent a player’s attempted interaction as input/command data and have a validation system return an explicit success/failure result before state is changed. On successful commit, publish facts such as `DoorRepaired`, `BreachSealed`, `CrewMemberDied`, or `EvacuationCompleted` for separate objective, log, and presentation readers. This gives independent readers a real use without making a goal tracker consume or block the action itself. Render/audio/UI effects should follow successful world commit, because Bendvy rollback does not undo external effects. See the local [typed event contract](../../../bendvy/docs/parity/typed-data-events.md) and [affine event contract](../../../bendvy/docs/parity/affine-events.md).

Use transaction failure for a meaningful consistency boundary only. A repair interaction might change a door’s condition, consume or wear a tool, update the air-zone status, and publish an informational result. If those changes are one registered operation, failure should preserve the old world and publish no “fixed” event; if the systems are deliberately separate, document the intermediate state and evaluate the goal only after all required phases. A snapshot can help inspect the final entity composition, but it is not automatically an execution checkpoint for event readers or pending work.

## A closed single-player slice

The smallest recognizable first slice is an **engineer’s emergency shift**, not an attempt to reproduce full SS14:

- One controllable engineer in a compact, two-room station map, with a maintenance tool holding one repair charge, an emergency suit, a separating bulkhead, one airlock, one APC/power machine, a small breach in the adjacent bay, and a waiting evac shuttle.
- Two independent faults are active at start: the breach drains its bay, while the APC has disabled the powered airlock. The player has two viable routes with the same fixed start. Spend the repair charge on the breach, then slowly crank the unpowered airlock and risk the timer; or close the bulkhead, use the suit to tolerate the leak, spend the charge on the APC, and leave through the powered airlock. The pressure, power, tool-charge, and shuttle indicators show the cost of each choice.
- The player uses concrete tool interactions on entities, sees pressure/power indicators change, and must board the shuttle before its fixed departure. **Win:** the engineer is aboard when evacuation departs and is alive. **Lose:** the engineer dies, or the shuttle leaves while the engineer is outside. The faults and clock make both outcomes visible and repeatable.

The loop preserves job-like station repair, tool use, equipment/inventory, environmental danger, powered machinery, and evacuation. SS14’s actual `RoundEndSystem` calls a round-ending path and dispatches an announcement, and it has a timed auto-call path for emergency shuttle; the proposed deterministic clock/boarding rule is a simpler single-player adaptation, not a description of the original win condition. ([`RoundEndSystem.cs`](https://github.com/space-wizards/space-station-14/blob/971889f18a6b5f14b926d139ba3910b2872e14de/Content.Server/RoundEnd/RoundEndSystem.cs#L286-L315), auto-call update at [lines 374–390](https://github.com/space-wizards/space-station-14/blob/971889f18a6b5f14b926d139ba3910b2872e14de/Content.Server/RoundEnd/RoundEndSystem.cs#L374-L390))

This would be recognizable as a **station-engineering emergency game**, though not as a full SS13/14 social round. That distinction should stay visible in the final selection. Crew chat, role-play, antagonist deception, open-ended jobs, and player-created social situations cannot be simulated by adding ECS components to one human-controlled character. The official SS14 description makes social role-play part of the target; the official [Getting Started guide](https://wiki.spacestation14.io/wiki/Getting_Started) explicitly frames the player as a crew member expected to cooperate and role-play with other players.

## Cost hidden outside ECS

Atmosphere is the largest warning. The original `/tg/station` maintainers’ [Atmospherics design note](https://github.com/tgstation/tgstation/blob/b3949ec3629836fa25e3e5732d5dcefd2dc04c4d/code/modules/atmospherics/Atmospherics.md#L33-L80) describes ordered pipe-network rebuilding and gas processing, machinery updates, active-tile gas movement, excited groups, pressure impulses, hotspots, superconductivity, and exposed-atom processing. It further explains that tiles sleep and wake, and that connected pipe devices share a gas mix with explicit ordering concerns ([same source, active tiles and pipelines](https://github.com/tgstation/tgstation/blob/b3949ec3629836fa25e3e5732d5dcefd2dc04c4d/code/modules/atmospherics/Atmospherics.md#L256-L287), [pipeline semantics](https://github.com/tgstation/tgstation/blob/b3949ec3629836fa25e3e5732d5dcefd2dc04c4d/code/modules/atmospherics/Atmospherics.md#L416-L435)). This is a domain solver with activation, topology, and numerical state; ECS alone does not provide it. Even SS14’s `AtmosphereSystem` runs on grid/map and node-group state and depends on physics, containers, lookup, and damage.

The official [Robust grids guide](https://docs.spacestation14.com/en/robust-toolbox/transform/grids.html) says the engine stores tilemaps as chunked grids and uses Box2D for collision. Bendvy provides no such spatial index, tile rendering, or collision solver by implication of its ECS APIs. A station game also needs an interaction/input loop, map and entity prototypes, inventory UI, indicators, sprite/animation output, authored content, and—in a faithful multiplayer version—server/client state replication. The Robust source discusses separate client and server entity IDs and `NetEntity` conversion, another reason not to mistake ECS presence for free networking ([Robust net-entities guide](https://docs.spacestation14.com/en/robust-toolbox/netcode/net-entities.html)).

For a first slice, replace the full solvers with an intentionally bounded model: static orthogonal grid and simple collision; two air zones with scalar pressure/oxygen plus one leak rate and one fan; one power flag or tiny wired graph; no movable atmos machinery, heat propagation, chemical reactions, fluid simulation, map editor, multiplayer, or general prototype authoring. Keep just enough feedback for the player to make a repair choice. If a full atmosphere solver is required before the player can win or lose, reject SS14 as the first-clone target and retain it only as the deeper ECS reference.

The full SS14 surface spans at least these distinct families: actor control, inventory/containment, tools/construction, damage/destruction, atmosphere, power, round objectives, and multiplayer/presentation. The proposed solo slice uses roughly six: movement, carrying, tool repair, damage, air/power, and evacuation. Its recognizable social round core is removed, and its two environment networks still require specialized host logic.

## Required objections

**Could one array-of-structs update loop implement this slice?** Yes. With one engineer, one tool, one breach, one machine, and a shuttle, an array of a dozen records plus a small state machine would be simpler. ECS earns its place as a reusable library consumer when repair, damage, exposure, power, inventory, and round-end logic are independently registered and their queries overlap on shared entities. The player is not helped by a faster or more fashionable data model in this tiny slice; the proof is that adding a new repairable machine or a new entity that can take damage does not add branches across every update pass.

**Is the complexity really in a domain solver or content?** Yes, especially for faithful atmosphere and power networks. A full station also depends on maps, prototypes, interactions, UI, and high-volume content. The ECS model should not claim credit for the pressure solver or rendering pipeline. Keep the vertical slice small enough that neither dominates.

**Does the reduction remain recognizable?** It remains recognizable as a station-emergency/engineering scenario: a crew job, repair tools, life support, machinery, and evacuation. It is not a faithful clone of the open-ended multiplayer social game. If retaining role-play and player-to-player emergent stories is a hard requirement, single-player does not satisfy it; a minimal multiplayer host would be an additional major capability absent from the slice.

**Are systems or API features being added only for coverage?** They should not be. Use buffered independent readers for post-commit objective/log/presentation reactions; use a direct result/command path for cancelable tool attempts; use relationships for actual containment/ownership/pipe links and test deletion cleanup. Snapshot/inspection should serve debugging or an end-of-run review, not create a pretend checkpoint. Transaction boundaries should protect a repair action’s world changes, not every click.

**Does the source prove ECS architecture?** For SS14, yes at the design level: its official ECS guide and code use data-oriented components, entity systems, component-filtered subscriptions, queries, and entity identifiers. The cited actual container TODO is also evidence that the implementation is hybrid and evolving. For original `/tg/station` SS13, the inspected component source shows signal-reactive DM components on parent datums and a legacy mob subsystem; calling it a modern ECS would be inaccurate.

**Who wins and loses?** The engineer makes a resource/time trade-off between sealing the room and restoring evacuation power, observes the effects through pressure/power/health cues, and wins only by boarding alive before departure. Death or missing the shuttle is an unambiguous loss. The remaining caveat is that this is a deliberately authored solo scenario, not the unconstrained social outcomes that give SS13/14 its range.

## Initial ordinal rating

Scores use the [selection contract](00-evaluation-contract.md): 0 absent, 1 weak, 2 visible, 3 central. These are reasoned judgments, not measurements. For “burden outside ECS,” 3 means that host and solver work materially define the game.

| Criterion | Rating | Reason |
|---|---:|---|
| Composition | 3 | Tools, people, doors, machinery, and hazards share capabilities instead of requiring one class per named object. |
| Intersecting queries | 3 | The same crew, door, machine, or item participates in movement, interaction, damage, containment, environment, and round systems. |
| Runtime composition changes | 3 | Repair, breaking, taking/dropping/containing items, death, and cleanup change which systems match. |
| Events and phases | 3 | Synchronous attempt/lifecycle interactions plus scheduled atmosphere/power/round updates expose meaningful ordering and event patterns. |
| Relationships and cleanup | 3 | Hands, containers, occupied shuttles, controlled bodies, and networks depend on explicit link lifecycle. |
| Fit of a first slice | 1 | A solo engineer emergency is finishable and clear, but does not retain multiplayer social role-play. |
| Burden outside ECS | 3 | Atmosphere, topology, collision, rendering, interaction UI, maps/content, and multiplayer are major services. |
| Novelty relative to arena | 3 | The slice adds inventory/containment, tool-target interaction, environmental/power systems, and evac objective logic to combat/movement/damage. |

**Initial read:** maximum-ECS showcase **very strong**; first Bendvy clone **conditional and scope-sensitive**. The recommendation should fall if the simplified air/power model stops feeling like a station, or if a recognizable slice cannot be completed before specialized solvers and social simulation take over.
