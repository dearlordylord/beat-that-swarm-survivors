# 27 — ECS literature calibration: composition breadth and query workload

## Scope and conclusion

This note checks whether two recent ECS papers support the candidate study's two plausible meanings of “maximum ECS”: broad gameplay composition across systems and entity families, or a regular workload of homogeneous queries with visible read/write and conflict structure. I verified the bibliographic records and read the available full texts, focusing on their models, query/schedule treatment, experimental setup, and stated limits. I also checked the current official Bevy ECS documentation as a framework-specific example. No game or Bendvy benchmark was run.

Neither paper defines a universal ECS-value score or ranks game genres. Together they support keeping at least two goals separate. ECS's modularity and changeability make gameplay composition breadth a defensible project objective; the literature's operational and performance concerns support repeated query-shaped work, component access, storage layout, and conflicting writes as a different objective. A game can be strong on one and weak on the other. Therefore, the SS14 recommendation is defensible as a conditional choice for **cross-family gameplay integration breadth**, but the papers do not establish it as the game that “maximizes ECS” in general or as the clearest data-oriented/query workload.

## What the papers actually support

**Composition and modularity are real ECS motivations, but not a game-breadth metric.** Redmond et al. define an ECS program in terms of loosely coupled transformations over a shared, non-hierarchical store, paired with queries that retrieve their inputs. They connect splitting state into components and behavior into systems with modularity and adaptation as requirements change. That makes it reasonable to ask whether one system can own a behavior across multiple entity families, and whether independently owned behaviors compose without a universal dispatcher. The paper does not define a count of gameplay mechanisms, relationships, or cross-family interactions as a measure of ECS quality. ([Redmond et al., Introduction and query model](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.15264#S1), [Core ECS query and system model](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.15264#S3))

**Regular data queries and declared access form a separate data-oriented workload studied by this literature.** Tasnim and Zhao formalize archetypes as groups of entities with the same component set and columnar storage. Their Tower Defense systems operate over query-matched component columns, with the movement example applying the same `Position + Velocity` update across matching entities. Their implementation describes systems by the components they read/write, and schedules systems according to access conflicts. The experiment compares OOP, AoS, single-threaded archetype SoA, and parallel archetype SoA for that one simulation. This is evidence for evaluating repeated homogeneous passes, data layout, structural synchronization, and access conflicts—not evidence that Tower Defense or any one candidate genre wins a cross-game ECS contest. ([Tasnim and Zhao, storage and implementation](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.14919#S4), [systems and queries](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.14919#S6), [performance study](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.14919#S7))

**Conflict topology is more precise than “number of systems.”** Redmond et al.'s determinism results are stated in terms of mutations' influence: independently invoked systems can safely act over distinct entities under stated conditions, and concurrent schedules are deterministic when their component-label influences are disjoint. Their paper then surveys five frameworks, including Bevy, and finds gaps between the concurrency permitted by Core ECS and what those implementations expose. An entity matched by several systems is evidence of multi-system participation, but not automatically a conflict: shared reads or writes to disjoint state can have different scheduling consequences. Thus the meaningful observation is which reads and writes overlap, on which entities/components, and what ordering or synchronization follows—not merely how many systems a game description names or how many queries match the same entity. These are formal results about their model and surveyed framework versions, not a claim that every ECS offers the same scheduler or that Bendvy runs systems concurrently. ([Redmond et al., deterministic concurrency](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.15264#S4), [framework survey](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.15264#S5))

Official Bevy documentation makes the distinction concrete for one implementation: `Query` selects component data and may filter with `With`/`Without`; mutable query access contributes to system access, and Bevy's scheduler can run systems in parallel when their accesses are compatible. This illustrates the query/access axis, but Bevy documentation is not a definition that every ECS must parallelize. ([Bevy Query documentation](https://docs.rs/bevy/latest/bevy/ecs/system/struct.Query.html), [Bevy system and schedule documentation](https://docs.rs/bevy/latest/bevy/ecs/system/index.html))

**Structural churn is relevant, but it is not a proxy for dynamic behavior.** Tasnim and Zhao make component/entity structural changes staged operations that synchronize before later systems see the new archetype state; their conclusion explicitly leaves richer query filters and entity relationships for future work. Redmond et al. distinguish owned component updates from insertions/deletions and deferred structural mutations because those operations have different concurrency consequences. Neither paper says that every rule change or status transition should be represented as a component insertion/removal. A resource or an enum/flags payload can change gameplay behavior without archetype migration. Score behavior change and membership churn separately, as [the component-granularity audit](23-component-granularity-audit.md) now does. ([Tasnim and Zhao, staged mutations and conclusion](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.14919#S6), [Redmond et al., mutation categories](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.15264#S5))

## Limits on transferring the results

The first paper's model and experiment are specifically archetype-oriented. Its Tower Defense comparison uses four representations (OOP, AoS ECS, archetype SoA ECS, and parallel archetype SoA ECS), one fixed update workload, and one hardware configuration. The paper says a broader scalability study across entity counts, maps, and system complexity is future work; it also identifies filtered-query semantics and entity relationships as future extensions to its formalization. The paper's own results therefore cannot establish how interaction-heavy station behavior, global graph solvers, or relation access compare against regular bulk queries. Nor do they qualify Bendvy's storage, speed, or scheduler.

The second paper gives a more general formal account of queries, schedules, mutations, and deterministic concurrency, but it is not a performance comparison of games. Its results depend on the Core ECS model and on the surveyed framework implementations. They help identify questions for a consumer's access model; they do not imply that a rich gameplay interaction graph is inherently “more ECS,” or that deterministic concurrency is present in a library that has not implemented it.

## Calibration for the candidate criteria and SS14 recommendation

Use separate, non-aggregated judgments for these dimensions:

1. **Gameplay composition breadth:** how many independently owned behaviors reuse capabilities across entity families, with meaningful system interaction, lifecycle, relations, and player-visible change. This is the dimension where SS14 has the strongest source-backed case among the current candidates.
2. **Query regularity and access shape:** how much of the loop consists of repeated filtered iteration over stable component shapes, how broadly systems reuse those shapes, and which component reads/writes conflict. A swarm, shapez-like processor simulation, or Tower Defense-like workload may be a clearer candidate for this dimension; that is a design hypothesis, not a paper's recommendation or a fact about original-game throughput.
3. **Structural transitions:** which events actually insert/remove components or create/destroy entities, versus updating existing values/resources. Do not equate this with the number of capabilities or with player-visible rule changes.
4. **Specialized solver share and first-slice cost:** keep grid, network, path, collision, and relation-target work visible as separate boundaries. Such work may still be integrated with ECS systems, but does not itself demonstrate a broad homogeneous query surface.

Accordingly, retain SS14 only as a **conditional recommendation for compositional/integration breadth**. Its original ECS docs and source make its architecture unusually inspectable, and the source study records many cross-system interaction seams ([SS14 study](07-space-station.md)). That evidence does not show that a compact station slice runs broad repeated queries, offers disjoint access patterns, or yields more data-oriented work than a bounded swarm/factory/defense loop. The existing [synthesis](REPORT.md) already treats swarm/shapez as a separate interpretation and keeps solver burden visible; this literature review strengthens that qualification rather than replacing the candidate ranking.

The criteria should not silently turn a modularity question into a throughput question, or vice versa. If the intended evaluation is gameplay breadth, SS14 can remain first while query regularity is reported as a separate unranked/conditional axis. If the intended evaluation is ordinary query/access semantics in Bendvy, choose and justify a workload with regular matching sets and legible access conflicts; SS14 need not lead. Neither conclusion authorizes claims about runtime performance. The project contract states the current Bendvy scheduler is sequential, so query/access regularity can be discussed as API and data-model structure, not as measured parallelism or speed. ([evaluation contract](00-evaluation-contract.md#привязка-к-текущему-проекту), [data-oriented counterargument](22-data-oriented-counterargument.md))
